<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Introduction:</span> The Pursuit of Happiness</h2>

<p>In 1776 a group of men signed their names under a sentence that said every person is endowed by their Creator with certain rights, and that among them are &ldquo;Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness.&rdquo; Notice what was promised. Not happiness. The <em>pursuit</em> of it. Nearly two hundred and fifty years later, we are still running. We chase it through promotions and paychecks, through relationships and vacations, through screens and substances and the next purchase. A whole industry now sells it by the chapter and the course. And most of us, if we are honest, are still not sure we have caught it.</p>

<p>The Bible does not scold us for wanting to be happy. That surprises some Christians. Many of us grew up hearing a line that sounds very spiritual: &ldquo;God doesn&rsquo;t want you happy. He wants you holy.&rdquo; There is a true warning inside that sentence. God will not trade your holiness for your comfort. But as a whole statement it is only half right, and the half it gets wrong has done real harm. It teaches people that holiness is the price you pay and happiness is the thing you give up. It makes God sound like the enemy of our joy.</p>

<p>The Puritan Thomas Watson saw it more clearly. &ldquo;Blessedness is the desire of all men,&rdquo; he wrote (<em>The Beatitudes</em>, ch. 2). The desire is not the problem. God made us to want it. The problem is where we look. &ldquo;Millions of men mistake both the nature of blessedness and the way thither&rdquo; (ibid.). So this study takes the world&rsquo;s search seriously, sets its favorite answers beside what God has said, and follows the Scriptures to a conclusion the older Reformed writers stated without embarrassment. In 1662 Thomas Brooks gave a whole book this title: <em>Holiness, the Only Way to Happiness</em>. He was not being clever. He was summarizing the Bible.</p>

<p>Three convictions run underneath every section. First, <strong>God is the good.</strong> He is not merely the giver of good things; he is the thing itself (Ps. 16:11; 73:25). Second, <strong>sin always lies about happiness.</strong> It promises joy and pays in ashes (Jer. 2:13; Heb. 11:25). Third, <strong>holiness is not the price of joy; it is the shape of it.</strong> To become like God is to share his happiness (Heb. 12:10; 1 Pet. 1:8, 15&ndash;16). The Westminster Shorter Catechism puts all three in one sentence: &ldquo;Man&rsquo;s chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him forever&rdquo; (Q. 1). Glorify and enjoy. Not one or the other.</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">The Word:</span> O the Happiness of That Man</h2>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">Psalm 1:1&ndash;3</span> &nbsp;&ldquo;Blessed is the man who walks not in the counsel of the wicked, nor stands in the way of sinners, nor sits in the seat of scoffers; but his delight is in the law of the LORD, and on his law he meditates day and night. He is like a tree planted by streams of water that yields its fruit in its season, and its leaf does not wither. In all that he does, he prospers.&rdquo;</div>

<p>The very first word of the Psalter is a word about happiness. Our English Bibles say &ldquo;Blessed,&rdquo; and that is a fine translation, but it can make the word sound stiff and churchy, like something said over you with a raised hand. The Hebrew word is warmer than that.</p>

<div class="sg-anatomy">
<div class="sg-head">אַשְׁרֵי <span class="sg-translit">(ʾashrê)</span></div>
<div class="sg-gloss">translated &ldquo;blessed.&rdquo; Happy, flourishing, well off; literally an exclamation: &ldquo;O the happiness of&hellip;&rdquo;</div>
<div class="sg-parts">
<div class="sg-part"><div class="sg-pg">אַשְׁרֵי (ʾashrê)</div><div class="sg-pt">Hebrew, H835</div><div class="sg-pd">A plural of fullness. Young&rsquo;s Literal Translation renders Psalm 1:1, &ldquo;O the happiness of that one.&rdquo; It is the first word of the Psalms and of Psalm 32.</div></div>
<div class="sg-plus">&rarr;</div>
<div class="sg-part"><div class="sg-pg">μακάριος (makarios)</div><div class="sg-pt">Greek, G3107</div><div class="sg-pd">The word the Greek Old Testament uses for <em>ʾashrê</em>, and the word Jesus uses nine times at the start of the Sermon on the Mount: &ldquo;Blessed are&hellip;&rdquo;</div></div>
</div>
<div class="sg-payoff"><b>Payoff:</b> This is a different word from the &ldquo;blessed&rdquo; that means &ldquo;spoken well of&rdquo; or &ldquo;praised&rdquo; (Hebrew <em>bārûk</em>, Greek <em>eulogētos</em>). <em>ʾAshrê</em> and <em>makarios</em> describe a condition: the person who is truly well off. So when Jesus opens his great sermon with &ldquo;Blessed are the poor in spirit&hellip; Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness&hellip; Blessed are the pure in heart&rdquo; (Matt. 5:3&ndash;8), he is answering the question every human heart is asking: <em>Who is actually happy?</em> And every answer he gives is a description of holiness. Paul even uses <em>makarios</em> of God himself: &ldquo;the gospel of the glory of the <em>blessed</em> God&rdquo; (1 Tim. 1:11). God is supremely happy in himself. To be made like him is to be brought into his happiness.</div>
</div>

<p>Look at what Psalm 1 says the happy man does and does not do. He does not take his counsel from the wicked, linger in the path of sinners, or settle into the scoffer&rsquo;s chair. Walk, stand, sit: sin slows you down until you are not going anywhere. Instead, &ldquo;his delight is in the law of the LORD.&rdquo; Not his duty. His delight. The holy life in Psalm 1 is not a man gritting his teeth through a list of rules. It is a man who has found where the water is and put his roots down beside it.</p>

<p>So the Bible&rsquo;s own vocabulary will not let us separate holiness from happiness. The word for the holy man <em>is</em> the word for the happy man.</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">The Broken Cisterns:</span> What the World Says vs. the Word</h2>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">Jeremiah 2:13</span> &nbsp;&ldquo;For my people have committed two evils: they have forsaken me, the fountain of living waters, and hewed out cisterns for themselves, broken cisterns that can hold no water.&rdquo;</div>

<p>In ancient Israel a cistern was a pit dug out of rock and plastered to hold rainwater. It was hard work, and it held flat, still water at best. A spring was the opposite: fresh water bubbling up on its own, free. Jeremiah&rsquo;s picture is almost comic. God&rsquo;s people walked away from a spring to go dig their own tanks, and the tanks were cracked. That is the world&rsquo;s pursuit of happiness in one sentence. It is not that people want too much. It is that they settle for leaky substitutes, and they work very hard to do it. &ldquo;They who go to the creature for blessedness,&rdquo; Watson wrote, &ldquo;go to the wrong box&rdquo; (<em>The Beatitudes</em>, ch. 2).</p>

<div class="sg-figure">
<img src="/assets/teaching-happiness-cisterns.jpg" alt="A dry and cracked stone cistern holding the world's abandoned treasures: a tipped goblet, scattered coins, a small crown, a laurel wreath, and a hand mirror">
<div class="sg-cap">&ldquo;Broken cisterns that can hold no water&rdquo; (Jer. 2:13). Every one of them was dug with great effort. None of them holds anything.</div>
</div>

<p>Here are six of the cisterns our culture digs most often, each set beside what God has said.</p>

<h3 class="sg-sign"><span class="sg-snum">1</span>Money</h3>

<div class="sg-vs"><div class="sg-feel"><span class="sg-lab">The world says</span>&ldquo;A little more and I&rsquo;ll be happy.&rdquo;</div><div class="sg-word"><span class="sg-lab">The Word says</span>Your life does not consist in the abundance of your possessions. Godliness with contentment is great gain.</div></div>

<p>Money is not evil. It is a gift, and the Bible is full of honest work and generous giving. But money is a terrible god, because it can never say &ldquo;enough.&rdquo; Jesus told a story about a farmer whose barns overflowed and who said to his soul, &ldquo;Relax, eat, drink, be merry.&rdquo; God called him a fool that same night (Luke 12:19&ndash;20). The wealth was real. The happiness it promised was not.</p>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">Luke 12:15</span> &nbsp;&ldquo;Take care, and be on your guard against all covetousness, for one&rsquo;s life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions.&rdquo;</div>

<p>Paul sets the alternative plainly: &ldquo;But godliness with contentment is great gain&rdquo; (1 Tim. 6:6). Godliness is holiness lived out. Contentment is happiness that no longer needs the next thing. Put them together and you are rich. Charles Spurgeon said it from the pulpit in 1877: &ldquo;A man will never be dazzled with gold, who has his heart satiated with God!&rdquo;</p>

<p class="sg-refs"><b>Also read:</b> Ecclesiastes 5:10; Matthew 6:19&ndash;21, 24; Philippians 4:11&ndash;13; 1 Timothy 6:6&ndash;10, 17&ndash;19; Hebrews 13:5</p>

<h3 class="sg-sign"><span class="sg-snum">2</span>Pleasure</h3>

<div class="sg-vs"><div class="sg-feel"><span class="sg-lab">The world says</span>&ldquo;If it feels good, it must be good for me.&rdquo;</div><div class="sg-word"><span class="sg-lab">The Word says</span>Sin&rsquo;s pleasures are fleeting. God&rsquo;s pleasures are forevermore.</div></div>

<p>Solomon ran this experiment further than any of us ever will. &ldquo;Whatever my eyes desired I did not keep from them. I kept my heart from no pleasure&rdquo; (Eccl. 2:10). He had the wine, the music, the gardens, the houses, the women. And his verdict at the end was this:</p>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">Ecclesiastes 2:11</span> &nbsp;&ldquo;Then I considered all that my hands had done and the toil I had expended in doing it, and behold, all was vanity and a striving after wind, and there was nothing to be gained under the sun.&rdquo;</div>

<p>The problem with sinful pleasure is not that it is too pleasurable. It is that it does not last and it does not satisfy. Moses understood this. He grew up in Pharaoh&rsquo;s palace with every pleasure Egypt could offer, and he walked away from it, &ldquo;choosing rather to be mistreated with the people of God than to enjoy the fleeting pleasures of sin&rdquo; (Heb. 11:25). Fleeting is the key word. Notice that Hebrews does not say Moses gave up pleasure. It says he traded a fleeting pleasure for a greater one: &ldquo;for he was looking to the reward&rdquo; (11:26). Compare Psalm 16:11. At God&rsquo;s right hand there are pleasures, and they are &ldquo;forevermore.&rdquo;</p>

<p class="sg-refs"><b>Also read:</b> Proverbs 14:12&ndash;13; Isaiah 55:1&ndash;2; Hebrews 11:24&ndash;26; James 4:1&ndash;4; 1 John 2:15&ndash;17</p>

<h3 class="sg-sign"><span class="sg-snum">3</span>Approval</h3>

<div class="sg-vs"><div class="sg-feel"><span class="sg-lab">The world says</span>&ldquo;If people admire me, I&rsquo;ll finally feel like I matter.&rdquo;</div><div class="sg-word"><span class="sg-lab">The Word says</span>Seek the glory that comes from the only God.</div></div>

<p>We are made for glory, so it is no surprise we hunger for it. But the approval of people is the leakiest cistern of all. It has to be refilled every day, and the crowd that cheers on Sunday can shout &ldquo;Crucify him&rdquo; on Friday. Jesus put his finger on why the religious leaders of his day could not believe in him: they were addicted to each other&rsquo;s praise.</p>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">John 5:44</span> &nbsp;&ldquo;How can you believe, when you receive glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from the only God?&rdquo;</div>

<p>The holy life frees you from the tyranny of the audience. When your heart is set on hearing &ldquo;Well done, good and faithful servant&rdquo; (Matt. 25:21), the opinion of the room loses its grip. That is not a loss of joy. It is a release into it.</p>

<p class="sg-refs"><b>Also read:</b> Proverbs 29:25; Galatians 1:10; Matthew 6:1&ndash;6; 1 Corinthians 4:3&ndash;5; 1 Thessalonians 2:4</p>

<h3 class="sg-sign"><span class="sg-snum">4</span>Comfort</h3>

<div class="sg-vs"><div class="sg-feel"><span class="sg-lab">The world says</span>&ldquo;Happiness is the absence of pain.&rdquo;</div><div class="sg-word"><span class="sg-lab">The Word says</span>God disciplines us for our good, that we may share his holiness, and it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness.</div></div>

<p>If happiness means nothing hard ever happens, then no one has ever been happy, including Jesus. J.C. Ryle was careful here: &ldquo;True happiness is not perfect freedom from sorrow and discomfort&rdquo; (<em>Happiness</em>, 1878). The Christian is not promised an easy life. He is promised a good Father who uses even the hard things to make him holy, and therefore to make him glad.</p>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">Hebrews 12:10&ndash;11</span> &nbsp;&ldquo;For they disciplined us for a short time as it seemed best to them, but he disciplines us for our good, that we may share his holiness. For the moment all discipline seems painful rather than pleasant, but later it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it.&rdquo;</div>

<p>Notice the order. Painful now, fruitful later. Holiness is the fruit, and peace comes with it. Paul could describe the Christian life as &ldquo;sorrowful, yet always rejoicing&rdquo; (2 Cor. 6:10). Both words are true at the same time. The world cannot hold them together. The gospel can.</p>

<p class="sg-refs"><b>Also read:</b> Romans 5:3&ndash;5; 8:28&ndash;29; 2 Corinthians 4:16&ndash;18; James 1:2&ndash;4; 1 Peter 1:6&ndash;9</p>

<h3 class="sg-sign"><span class="sg-snum">5</span>Freedom</h3>

<div class="sg-vs"><div class="sg-feel"><span class="sg-lab">The world says</span>&ldquo;I&rsquo;ll be happy when nobody tells me what to do.&rdquo;</div><div class="sg-word"><span class="sg-lab">The Word says</span>Everyone who practices sin is a slave to sin. If the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed.</div></div>

<p>We think of holiness as a cage and sin as the open field. Jesus says it is the other way around. Sin is the cage. It promises freedom and delivers addiction, shame, and broken relationships. Anyone who has tried to stop a sin in his own strength knows how free he really was.</p>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">John 8:34, 36</span> &nbsp;&ldquo;Truly, truly, I say to you, everyone who practices sin is a slave to sin&hellip; So if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed.&rdquo;</div>

<p>Real freedom is not the absence of a master. It is having the right one. &ldquo;Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light&rdquo; (Matt. 11:29&ndash;30). The psalmist found the commandments to be a wide-open road: &ldquo;Lead me in the path of your commandments, for I delight in it&rdquo; (Ps. 119:35).</p>

<p class="sg-refs"><b>Also read:</b> Romans 6:16&ndash;23; Galatians 5:1, 13; Psalm 119:32, 45; 2 Peter 2:19</p>

<h3 class="sg-sign"><span class="sg-snum">6</span>The Self</h3>

<div class="sg-vs"><div class="sg-feel"><span class="sg-lab">The world says</span>&ldquo;Follow your heart. Be true to yourself.&rdquo;</div><div class="sg-word"><span class="sg-lab">The Word says</span>The heart is deceitful above all things. Whoever loses his life for my sake will find it.</div></div>

<p>This is the cistern underneath all the others. Money, pleasure, approval, comfort, and freedom are usually just different ways of serving the self. And the self is a bottomless pit. It cannot be filled, because it was never meant to be the source. It was meant to be a vessel.</p>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">Matthew 16:25</span> &nbsp;&ldquo;For whoever would save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake will find it.&rdquo;</div>

<p>Henry Scougal, a young Scottish minister, wrote in 1677 that &ldquo;the worth and excellency of a soul is to be measured by the object of its love&rdquo; (<em>The Life of God in the Soul of Man</em>). A soul that loves only itself shrinks to its own size. A soul that loves God grows toward his. Jesus does not ask you to stop wanting life. He tells you where to find it.</p>

<p class="sg-refs"><b>Also read:</b> Jeremiah 17:9; Proverbs 3:5&ndash;7; 28:26; Galatians 2:20; Philippians 3:7&ndash;11</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Holiness,</span> the Only Way to Happiness</h2>

<p>Once we see the cisterns for what they are, the Bible&rsquo;s positive answer comes into focus. Happiness is not found <em>beside</em> God, as if he were one more good thing among others. It is found <em>in</em> God. And holiness is simply the way a sinner is brought near enough to enjoy him.</p>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">Psalm 73:25&ndash;26</span> &nbsp;&ldquo;Whom have I in heaven but you? And there is nothing on earth that I desire besides you. My flesh and my heart may fail, but God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever.&rdquo;</div>

<p>Asaph wrote those words after nearly losing his faith over the happiness of the wicked. They had money, health, and no troubles, and he envied them (Ps. 73:3&ndash;12). Then he went into the sanctuary of God, and he saw their end, and he saw his own portion. The prosperous sinner has everything except God. The struggling saint has nothing except God, which turns out to be everything.</p>

<h3 class="sg-h3">God is the fountain</h3>

<p>Jonathan Edwards preached a sermon in 1733 called &ldquo;The Christian Pilgrim.&rdquo; In it he said that &ldquo;God is the highest good of the reasonable creature; and the enjoyment of him is the only happiness with which our souls can be satisfied.&rdquo; Then he gave one of the most beautiful pictures in all of Puritan and Reformed writing. All the good things of this life, he said, family and friends and earthly comforts, &ldquo;are but shadows. But the enjoyment of God is the substance. These are but scattered beams, but God is the sun. These are but streams, but God is the fountain. These are but drops, but God is the ocean&rdquo; (<em>Works</em>, Banner of Truth, 2:244).</p>

<div class="sg-figure">
<img src="/assets/teaching-happiness-ocean.jpg" alt="Small mountain streams running down a valley into a wide calm ocean as the sun rises over the water">
<div class="sg-cap">&ldquo;These are but streams, but God is the fountain. These are but drops, but God is the ocean&rdquo; (Jonathan Edwards). The world&rsquo;s joys are not lies when they lead us to him. They become lies when they try to replace him.</div>
</div>

<p>That changes how we enjoy everything else. A Christian can love his wife, laugh with his friends, and enjoy a good meal more freely than anyone, because none of those things has to be God for him. They are streams. He knows where the fountain is.</p>

<h3 class="sg-h3">Holiness is the road</h3>

<p>But a sinner cannot walk into the presence of a holy God and enjoy him. &ldquo;Strive for peace with everyone, and for the holiness without which no one will see the Lord&rdquo; (Heb. 12:14). Jesus said the same thing in the Beatitudes: &ldquo;Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God&rdquo; (Matt. 5:8). That verse was one of the texts on the title page of Brooks&rsquo;s book, and his whole argument is packed into it. The happiness is seeing God. The way there is a pure heart. Brooks put it bluntly: &ldquo;So that there is now no way under heaven to be happy&mdash;but by being holy.&rdquo; And again, turning it around to show God&rsquo;s intent: &ldquo;where he is resolved to make happy, there he is resolved to make holy&rdquo; (<em>The Crown and Glory of Christianity</em>, 1662, Epistle Dedicatory).</p>

<p>Jesus himself tied obedience and joy together in the upper room, on the night before he died:</p>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">John 15:10&ndash;11</span> &nbsp;&ldquo;If you keep my commandments, you will abide in my love, just as I have kept my Father&rsquo;s commandments and abide in his love. These things I have spoken to you, that my joy may be in you, and that your joy may be full.&rdquo;</div>

<p>Read it slowly. Keeping his commandments is how we abide in his love. And the reason he tells us this is joy, his joy in us, and our joy made full. Holiness is not a detour from joy. It is the road that runs straight into it.</p>

<p>The Reformed confessions say the same thing in their own careful way. The Heidelberg Catechism describes true conversion as two things: &ldquo;the dying of the old self and the birth of the new&rdquo; (Q. 88). And what is the new self? &ldquo;Complete joy in God through Christ and a strong desire to live according to the will of God in all good works&rdquo; (Q. 90). Notice that joy in God and the desire for holiness are not two separate blessings. They are one new heart. The Westminster Confession adds that the believer&rsquo;s assurance enlarges his heart &ldquo;in peace and joy in the Holy Ghost, in love and thankfulness to God, and in strength and cheerfulness in the duties of obedience&rdquo; (WCF 18.3). Cheerful obedience. That is what holiness looks like from the inside.</p>

<p>J.C. Ryle, the Anglican bishop whose book <em>Holiness</em> is still read today, summed it up in six words: &ldquo;The holiest men are always the happiest!&rdquo; (<em>Happiness</em>, 1878).</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">The Expulsive Power:</span> How the Change Happens</h2>

<p>So far this may sound like a simple instruction: stop drinking from cisterns and go to the fountain. But anyone who has tried knows it is not that simple. The cistern is right there, and it is familiar, and it still tastes like water. How does a heart actually let go?</p>

<p>In the early 1800s a Scottish preacher named Thomas Chalmers answered that question in a sermon on 1 John 2:15 (&ldquo;Do not love the world or the things in the world&rdquo;). You cannot simply rip a love out of the human heart, he said, and leave an empty space. The heart will not stay empty. It must love something. Telling people how bad the world is will not do it. What will? &ldquo;The only way to dispossess it of an old affection, is by the expulsive power of a new one&rdquo; (&ldquo;The Expulsive Power of a New Affection&rdquo;).</p>

<p>That is how holiness grows. Not mainly by willpower, though effort is real (Phil. 2:12&ndash;13). It grows when something better takes the place of the thing we loved. Jesus told a two-sentence parable about exactly this:</p>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">Matthew 13:44</span> &nbsp;&ldquo;The kingdom of heaven is like treasure hidden in a field, which a man found and covered up. Then in his joy he goes and sells all that he has and buys that field.&rdquo;</div>

<div class="sg-figure">
<img src="/assets/teaching-happiness-treasure.jpg" alt="A man kneeling in a plowed field, lifting the lid of a buried chest as gold light spills out onto his face">
<div class="sg-cap">&ldquo;Then in his joy he goes and sells all that he has&rdquo; (Matt. 13:44). Nobody pities this man. He is not giving things up. He is trading up.</div>
</div>

<p>Three words carry the whole parable: &ldquo;in his joy.&rdquo; The man sells everything, which sounds like the ultimate sacrifice. But Jesus says he does it gladly, because he has seen what is in that field. That is the secret of the holy life. It is not mainly a life of saying no. It is a life of having seen something so good that the no takes care of itself.</p>

<p>John Piper has spent his ministry pressing this point, and he puts it in one sentence: &ldquo;God is most glorified in us when we are most satisfied in him&rdquo; (<em>Desiring God</em>). It follows that every temptation is a contest between two promises of happiness. Sin says, &ldquo;You will be happier with me.&rdquo; God says, &ldquo;In my presence there is fullness of joy.&rdquo; We fight sin by believing the better promise. John Owen, the greatest of the Puritan theologians, saw the same battle and named it from the other side: &ldquo;where there is a predominant satisfaction in these things, there is no spiritual joy&rdquo; (<em>Works</em> 17:588). The question is never whether you will be satisfied by something. The question is which satisfaction will rule your heart.</p>

<p>Spurgeon saw this as one of the great safeguards of the Christian life: &ldquo;God&rsquo;s people will not go astray from him, when their hearts are thoroughly happy with him&rdquo; (&ldquo;Happiness the Privilege and Duty of Christians,&rdquo; June 10, 1877). Happy saints are hard to tempt. That is why joy in God is not optional. It is a weapon.</p>

<p>R.C. Sproul once described his own years of chasing the next goal: &ldquo;I kept changing my goals and my plans and thinking that each plateau would bring me final peace and happiness.&rdquo; Each plateau came. The peace did not. His conclusion was Augustine&rsquo;s, and it is ours: &ldquo;we cannot be satisfied until we drink of the water that is Christ&rdquo; (&ldquo;Satisfied in Christ,&rdquo; Ligonier Ministries).</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">The Turn:</span> The Joy Set Before Him</h2>

<div class="sg-figure">
<img src="/assets/teaching-happiness-cross.jpg" alt="An empty wooden cross on a rocky hill at dawn with a narrow path climbing toward it and gold light breaking behind">
<div class="sg-cap">&ldquo;Who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross&rdquo; (Heb. 12:2). The holiest man who ever lived was also the one most set on joy.</div>
</div>

<p>Everything in this study could still leave you staring at yourself. Am I holy enough to be happy? Have I found enough joy to fight my sin? If that is where it ends, it is just one more cistern, a more religious one. So we have to finish where Scripture always finishes: with Christ.</p>

<p>First, look at <strong>what Jesus pursued.</strong> He is the only perfectly holy man who has ever lived, and Hebrews tells us what drove him to the cross:</p>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">Hebrews 12:1&ndash;2</span> &nbsp;&ldquo;&hellip;let us run with endurance the race that is set before us, looking to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of our faith, who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is seated at the right hand of the throne of God.&rdquo;</div>

<p>For the joy. The most costly act of obedience in the history of the world was done by a man who was looking at joy: the joy of his Father&rsquo;s glory, the joy of his bride redeemed, the joy of the right hand of God where, Psalm 16 says, there are pleasures forevermore. If anyone ever proved that holiness and happiness are one road, it was Jesus.</p>

<p>Second, look at <strong>where happiness begins.</strong> David wrote Psalm 32 after his worst failure, the adultery and murder that nearly wrecked his life. And he opens it with the same word that opens Psalm 1:</p>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">Psalm 32:1&ndash;2</span> &nbsp;&ldquo;Blessed is the one whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered. Blessed is the man against whom the LORD counts no iniquity, and in whose spirit there is no deceit.&rdquo;</div>

<p><em>ʾAshrê</em> again. O the happiness of the forgiven man. Paul quotes these verses in Romans 4:7&ndash;8 to show that this happiness comes by grace, through faith, not by works. That matters enormously. Holiness is the road to happiness, but no sinner can start down that road on his own. We start at the cross, forgiven. Before David could sing about delight in God&rsquo;s law, he had to confess what he had done: &ldquo;I acknowledged my sin to you&hellip; and you forgave the iniquity of my sin&rdquo; (Ps. 32:5). The first happiness of the Christian life is a God who no longer counts your sins against you. Every other joy grows from that root.</p>

<p>Third, look at <strong>who makes you holy.</strong> The Shorter Catechism defines sanctification as &ldquo;the work of God&rsquo;s free grace, whereby we are renewed in the whole man after the image of God, and are enabled more and more to die unto sin, and live unto righteousness&rdquo; (Q. 35). Free grace. God&rsquo;s work. The Spirit who produces holiness in us is the same Spirit whose fruit is &ldquo;love, joy, peace&rdquo; (Gal. 5:22). He does not hand you a list and wish you luck. He changes what you love. &ldquo;And we all, with unveiled face, beholding the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another&rdquo; (2 Cor. 3:18). We become like what we behold. Behold him, and you will become holy. Become holy, and you will see him more. See him more, and you will be glad.</p>

<p>The world pursues happiness and never catches it, because it is running away from the only One who has it to give. The Christian stops running from God and runs to him, and finds that the road home was the road to joy all along. As Thomas Watson put it, &ldquo;the same day you become godly, you become blessed&rdquo; (<em>The Beatitudes</em>, ch. 3).</p>

<h3 class="sg-h3">Living It: Five Practices</h3>

<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">1</span><strong>Name your cisterns.</strong> Look back over the six in this study. Which one do you run to first when life is hard? Write it down, and ask God to show you how it has failed to hold water (Ps. 139:23&ndash;24).</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">2</span><strong>Drink before you work.</strong> George M&uuml;ller made it his first business every morning to get his soul happy in the Lord before he did anything else. Start the day in the Word and in prayer, not to check a box, but to drink from the fountain before the cisterns start calling (Ps. 90:14).</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">3</span><strong>Fight a temptation with a better promise.</strong> When sin offers you happiness, answer it with a specific promise of God. Memorize one this week. Psalm 16:11 is a good place to start (Ps. 119:11).</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">4</span><strong>Confess quickly.</strong> Hidden sin drains joy. &ldquo;When I kept silent, my bones wasted away&rdquo; (Ps. 32:3). Do not wait. Bring it into the light, receive his forgiveness, and ask with David, &ldquo;Restore to me the joy of your salvation&rdquo; (Ps. 51:12; 1 John 1:9).</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">5</span><strong>Rejoice on purpose.</strong> &ldquo;Rejoice in the Lord always; again I will say, rejoice&rdquo; (Phil. 4:4). Joy is commanded, which means it can be pursued. Sing with God&rsquo;s people on the Lord&rsquo;s Day. Thank him out loud for three specific things each night. &ldquo;The joy of the LORD is your strength&rdquo; (Neh. 8:10).</div>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">For Discussion</span></h2>

<ol>
<li>Have you ever heard &ldquo;God wants you holy, not happy&rdquo;? What is true about that saying, and what does Psalm 1 or Matthew 5 add to it?</li>
<li>Which of the six cisterns (money, pleasure, approval, comfort, freedom, the self) pulls hardest at you? How has it failed to hold water?</li>
<li>Thomas Watson said those who &ldquo;bless themselves in their sins&rdquo; will never be blessed. Think of a specific sin. What happiness did it promise you, and what did it actually deliver?</li>
<li>Thomas Chalmers says an old love is driven out only by a new one. What would a &ldquo;better promise&rdquo; look like in a temptation you are facing right now?</li>
<li>Psalm 32 begins, &ldquo;O the happiness of the one whose transgression is forgiven.&rdquo; Why does Christian happiness have to begin with forgiveness instead of with our own holiness?</li>
<li>Jesus endured the cross &ldquo;for the joy that was set before him&rdquo; (Heb. 12:2). How does his example change the way you think about obedience that costs you something?</li>
</ol>
