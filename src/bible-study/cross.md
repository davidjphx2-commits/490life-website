<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Introduction:</span> The Lamb God Provided</h2>

<p>In Lesson 6, Isaac asked his father a question on the way up Mount Moriah: “where is the lamb for a burnt offering?” Abraham answered, “God will provide for himself the lamb” (Gen. 22:7–8). Ever since, the story has been full of lambs. The Passover lamb whose blood marked the doors in Egypt (Lesson 8). The lambs on the altar, morning and evening, for well over a thousand years. The goat for the LORD on the Day of Atonement (Lesson 11). The Servant in Isaiah “like a lamb that is led to the slaughter” (Isa. 53:7). And John the Baptist pointing at Jesus: “Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!” (John 1:29).</p>

<p>This lesson is about the day the Lamb was offered. It is the center of the whole Bible. The four Gospels spend roughly a third of their pages on the last week of Jesus’ life. Jesus told his disciples again and again that “the Son of Man must suffer many things and be rejected… and be killed, and after three days rise again” (Mark 8:31). He “set his face to go to Jerusalem” (Luke 9:51), knowing exactly what waited there.</p>

<p>On our timeline, Jesus was crucified in Jerusalem at Passover, around AD 30 or 33 (scholars debate the exact year). We will walk through his last night and his death, and then ask the most important question in this whole study: what happened on the cross, and was it for you?</p>

<div class="sg-payoff"><b>Payoff:</b> A dying man might say, “I am finished.” Jesus said, “It is finished,” one word in Greek, like a worker laying down his tools with the job complete. Everything the Father gave him to do was done. The payment for sin was made in full. So Hebrews sets two pictures side by side: “every priest stands daily at his service, offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins. <h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Part One:</span> The Last Supper</h2>

<p>On the night before he died, Jesus ate the Passover meal with his twelve disciples in an upstairs room in Jerusalem. For well over a thousand years, Israel had eaten this meal to remember the lamb whose blood saved them in Egypt. Now Jesus gave it a new meaning:</p>

<div class="sg-figure sg-small">
<img src="/assets/whole-story-21-supper.jpg" alt="Jesus at a low table with his twelve disciples, lifting a broken loaf of bread, with a cup before him and oil lamps glowing">
<div class="sg-cap">“This is my body, which is given for you” (Luke 22:19).</div>
</div>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">Luke 22:19–20</span> &nbsp;“And he took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it and gave it to them, saying, ‘This is my body, which is given for you. Do this in remembrance of me.’ And likewise the cup after they had eaten, saying, ‘This cup that is poured out for you is the new covenant in my blood.’”</div>

<p>Jesus was saying: I am the Passover Lamb. My body will be broken, my blood will be poured out, and it will be for you. “For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed” (1 Cor. 5:7). And with his blood he would establish the new covenant God promised through Jeremiah in the ashes of Jerusalem (Lesson 17). Christians still eat this meal, the Lord’s Supper. It is a memorial, but more than a memorial. In it, as the Westminster Shorter Catechism says, the worthy receivers “are, not after a corporal and carnal manner, but by faith, made partakers of his body and blood, with all his benefits, to their spiritual nourishment, and growth in grace” (WSC 96).</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Part Two:</span> The Cup in the Garden</h2>

<p>After supper, Jesus walked out to an olive grove called Gethsemane. He “began to be greatly distressed and troubled. And he said to them, ‘My soul is very sorrowful, even to death’” (Mark 14:33–34). Falling to the ground, he prayed: “Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will” (14:36).</p>

<div class="sg-figure sg-small">
<img src="/assets/whole-story-21-garden.jpg" alt="At night in an olive grove, Jesus kneels beside a large rock praying while three disciples sleep in the background and torches approach from the city">
<div class="sg-cap">“Yet not what I will, but what you will” (Mark 14:36).</div>
</div>

<p>What was the cup? In the Old Testament, “the cup” is a picture of God’s wrath against sin. Isaiah speaks of Jerusalem drinking “from the hand of the LORD the cup of his wrath” (Isa. 51:17), and God told Jeremiah, “Take from my hand this cup of the wine of wrath” (Jer. 25:15). The nails were real, but they were not the deepest thing he faced. He was facing the full weight of God’s judgment against the sins of his people. And he chose to drink it.</p>

<p>Soldiers came with torches and arrested him, and “they all left him and fled” (Mark 14:50). Through the night he was put on trial. The high priest asked him, “Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?” Jesus answered, “I am” (14:61–62), and they condemned him for blasphemy. In the morning they brought him to the Roman governor, Pontius Pilate. Pilate offered the crowd a choice: he would release one prisoner, Jesus or a rebel and murderer named Barabbas (Luke 23:19). The crowd chose Barabbas. “So Pilate, wishing to satisfy the crowd, released for them Barabbas, and having scourged Jesus, he delivered him to be crucified” (15:15). The guilty man walked free, and the innocent one went to the cross in his place.</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Part Three:</span> The Cross</h2>

<p>They crucified him at a place called The Skull, Golgotha, just outside Jerusalem’s walls, between two criminals (Luke 23:33; John 19:17, 20; Heb. 13:12). Crucifixion was the most shameful death Rome could give, reserved for slaves and rebels. Luke records Jesus praying for the people killing him: “Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do” (23:34). The crowds mocked: “He saved others; he cannot save himself” (Mark 15:31). They were more right than they knew. He could not save himself and save us too.</p>

<p>One of the criminals beside him mocked him too. The other told him to stop, because “this man has done nothing wrong.” Then he turned to Jesus: “Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom.” And Jesus said, “Truly, I say to you, today you will be with me in paradise” (Luke 23:41–43). A dying thief, who admitted he deserved his sentence and had no time to do a single good work, was saved through faith in Jesus alone.</p>

<p>At noon, darkness covered the land for three hours. Then, at about three in the afternoon, “Jesus cried with a loud voice, ‘Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani?’ which means, ‘My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?’” (Mark 15:34). He was quoting the first line of Psalm 22, a psalm David wrote a thousand years earlier that describes pierced hands and feet and men casting lots for clothing (Ps. 22:16–18). In that darkness, as our substitute, he bore the Father’s just wrath against our sin. He felt the full weight of being forsaken, cut off from every comfort of his Father’s favor. Yet the Trinity was never torn apart. He was still the beloved Son, and he cried “My God” even there.</p>

<p>Then he cried out, “It is finished” (John 19:30), and, “Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!” (Luke 23:46), and he breathed his last. At that very moment, “the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom” (Mark 15:38). The Roman officer who had supervised the execution said, “Truly this man was the Son of God!” (15:39). That evening a rich man named Joseph of Arimathea laid Jesus’ body in his own new tomb and rolled a great stone across the entrance (Matt. 27:57–60).</p>

<p>About seven hundred years before the crucifixion, Isaiah described a Servant of the LORD who would suffer. Read Isaiah 53 next to the Gospels and the details line up with startling precision.</p>

<p>But the most important thing Isaiah tells us is not the details. It is the meaning. “He was pierced for <em>our</em> transgressions.… the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all” (Isa. 53:5–6). The New Testament explains the cross with several pictures, each showing a different side of the same diamond.</p>

<p>At the center of them all is <strong>substitution</strong>: Jesus in our place. “He himself bore our sins in his body on the tree” (1 Pet. 2:24). “For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God” (2 Cor. 5:21). This is how God can be both perfectly just and the one who forgives. He does not ignore sin. He punishes it fully, in his Son, so that he can be “just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus” (Rom. 3:26). The Bible’s word for this is propitiation: a sacrifice that turns away God’s wrath by bearing it. And this was love, not grudging payment: “In this is love, not that we have loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins” (1 John 4:10).</p>

<p>Who killed Jesus? Peter told a crowd in Jerusalem, “this Jesus, delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God, you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men” (Acts 2:23). God planned it. Human beings were guilty of it. And Jesus went willingly: “No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord” (John 10:18).</p>

<div class="sg-thread-row"><span class="sg-thread-tag sg-lamb">The Lamb</span> The thread we have followed since Genesis reaches its goal. God provided the Lamb (Gen. 22:8). Like the Passover lamb, not one of his bones was broken (John 19:36; Ex. 12:46). Like the Day of Atonement, his blood answered God’s justice and his death carried our sins away. The way back to God is open.</div>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">The Turn:</span> For Me</h2>

<p>It is possible to know all of this as history and miss the heart of it. The apostle Paul did not miss it. He wrote, “the life I now live in the flesh I live by faith in the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me” (Gal. 2:20). Not just for the world in general. For me.</p>

<p>“God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us” (Rom. 5:8). He did not wait for you to clean up. He did not die for people who had cleaned themselves up. He died for sinners, for the ungodly, for his enemies. And because his work is finished, there is nothing left for you to pay. You cannot add to “It is finished.” You can only receive it.</p>

<p>The thief on the cross had nothing to bring. He could not be baptized, join a church, or do a single good deed. He simply turned to Jesus and said, “Jesus, remember me.” And Jesus said, “today you will be with me in paradise.” If you have never done it, you can do the same thing right now: turn from your sin, and trust the Lamb who died for sinners. No one who comes to him is turned away (John 6:37), and everyone who trusts him can say with Paul that the Son of God “loved me and gave himself for me.” And for everyone who does, this is the verdict: “There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus” (Rom. 8:1).</p>

<h3 class="sg-h3">Living It: Six Practices for This Week</h3>

<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">1</span><strong>Read the passion.</strong> Read Mark 14–15 in one sitting. Then read Isaiah 53 and notice how much was written seven centuries in advance.</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">2</span><strong>Make it personal.</strong> If you have trusted Christ, read Galatians 2:20 aloud and put your own name in it. If you have not, read it as an invitation.</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">3</span><strong>Pray in Gethsemane.</strong> Bring one hard thing to God this week and pray Jesus’ prayer: “Not what I will, but what you will.”</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">4</span><strong>Forgive.</strong> Jesus prayed for the people killing him. Is there someone you need to forgive because you have been forgiven?</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">5</span><strong>Come to the table.</strong> If you are a baptized believer and a member of a church, come to the Lord’s Supper with fresh gratitude for what it shows and gives. If you are not there yet, talk with a pastor about what it means to come.</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">6</span><strong>Memorize Isaiah 53:5.</strong> “But he was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and with his wounds we are healed.”</div>

<p class="sg-refs"><b>Before Lesson 22:</b> Read Mark 16, Luke 24, 1 Corinthians 15:1–28, and Acts 1:1–11. Lesson 22 opens the tomb.</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">For Discussion</span></h2>

<ol>
<li>Trace the lamb thread from Genesis 22 to the cross. Which link in that chain means the most to you now?</li>
<li>Jesus called the bread his body and the cup the new covenant in his blood. What does the Lord’s Supper show us about the cross?</li>
<li>What was “the cup” Jesus asked the Father to remove? Why did he drink it anyway?</li>
<li>Barabbas went free while Jesus was condemned. How is that a picture of what Jesus does for us?</li>
<li>The thief on the cross was saved with no time to do good works. What does that teach about how anyone is saved?</li>
<li>Look at the pictures of the cross: substitution, propitiation, redemption, reconciliation, victory. Which one do you need most right now?</li>
<li>Paul wrote, “who loved me and gave himself for me.” What would change if you really believed that about yourself?</li>
</ol>
