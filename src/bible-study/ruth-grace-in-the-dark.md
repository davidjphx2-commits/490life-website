<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Introduction:</span> Grace in the Dark</h2>

<p>Last week we watched Israel spiral downward through the days of the judges: sin, trouble, crying out, rescue, and back again. God raised up Othniel, Deborah, Gideon, and Samson, and every one of them saved Israel for a while. None of them could change Israel’s heart.</p>

<p>This week we see how dark it got when everyone did what was right in his own eyes. Then, in the same dark years, the Bible tells a quiet story about a Moabite widow, a bitter mother-in-law, and a kind farmer who was willing to pay.</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">The Word:</span> Redeemer</h2>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">Ruth 2:20</span> &nbsp;“And Naomi said to her daughter-in-law, ‘May he be blessed by the LORD, whose kindness has not forsaken the living or the dead!’ Naomi also said to her, ‘The man is a close relative of ours, one of our redeemers.’”</div>

<div class="sg-anatomy">
<div class="sg-head">גֹּאֵל <span class="sg-translit">(go’el, “redeemer”)</span></div>
<div class="sg-gloss">a family member who steps in to rescue a relative in trouble</div>
<div class="sg-parts">
<div class="sg-part"><div class="sg-pg">Buys back land</div><div class="sg-pt">Lev. 25:25</div><div class="sg-pd">If a poor relative had to sell his family land, the redeemer bought it back.</div></div>
<div class="sg-part"><div class="sg-pg">Buys back people</div><div class="sg-pt">Lev. 25:47–49</div><div class="sg-pd">If a poor relative sold himself as a servant to a foreigner, the redeemer paid to set him free.</div></div>
<div class="sg-part"><div class="sg-pg">Keeps a name alive</div><div class="sg-pt">Deut. 25:5–6</div><div class="sg-pd">If a man died childless, his brother was to marry the widow so the dead man’s name would not be blotted out. Ruth 4 applies the heart of this law to a more distant relative.</div></div>
</div>
<div class="sg-payoff"><b>Payoff:</b> Put the redeemer laws and Ruth’s story together and you see three things a redeemer needed to be: a close relative, able to pay, and willing to pay. The same word is used of God himself: “your Maker is your husband… and the Holy One of Israel is your Redeemer” (Isa. 54:5). Keep those three requirements in mind. They will lead us straight to Jesus.</div>
</div>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Part Three:</span> Right in His Own Eyes</h2>

<p>The last chapters of Judges stop following the judges and show what ordinary life had become. A man named Micah keeps idols made from silver his mother dedicated “to the LORD” (17:3), sets up a private shrine, and hires a wandering Levite as his priest. A whole tribe steals that priest and his idols. And the book ends with a crime and a civil war so terrible that they are among the darkest chapters in the Bible (Judg. 19–21). Four times the writer says “there was no king in Israel,” and the book ends on the full line:</p>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">Judges 21:25</span> &nbsp;“In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did what was right in his own eyes.”</div>

<p>That sentence sounds almost modern. “Do what is right for you.” Judges shows where it leads. When everyone becomes his own king, the weak get crushed. The book is crying out for a king who will do what is right in God’s eyes and lead his people to do the same.</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Part Four:</span> Ruth: Grace in the Dark</h2>

<p>Now turn the page to Ruth. A famine hits Bethlehem, and a man named Elimelech takes his wife Naomi and their two sons to Moab, a pagan nation east of the Dead Sea. Elimelech dies there. The sons marry Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth. About ten years later, both sons die too. Naomi is left with nothing: no husband, no sons, no grandchildren, in a foreign land.</p>

<p>When Naomi hears that God has given food to Bethlehem again, she starts home and tells her daughters-in-law to go back to their own families. Orpah kisses her and goes. But Ruth clings to her and says some of the most beautiful words in the Bible:</p>

<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">Ruth 1:16</span> &nbsp;“For where you go I will go, and where you lodge I will lodge. Your people shall be my people, and your God my God.”</div>

<p>This is more than loyalty to a mother-in-law. It is conversion. Ruth leaves her gods for Naomi’s God. And remember who she is: a Moabite. God’s law said, “No Ammonite or Moabite may enter the assembly of the LORD” (Deut. 23:3). Ruth is the ultimate outsider. The law shut Moabites out of the assembly because Moab had met Israel with curses instead of bread (Deut. 23:4). Ruth comes the other way, as a Moabite who blesses Israel and takes refuge under Israel’s God. The prophets later promised that foreigners who hold fast to the LORD would be welcomed into his house (Isa. 56:3–7). Ruth is an early taste of that promise. She is coming in by faith.</p>

<p>Back in Bethlehem, Naomi is bitter. “Do not call me Naomi” (the name means “pleasant”); “call me Mara” (“bitter”), “for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me. I went away full, and the LORD has brought me back empty” (1:20–21). She is honest about her pain, and the book does not scold her for it. But she is wrong about one thing. She is not empty. She has Ruth, and God is already at work.</p>

<div class="sg-figure sg-small">
<img src="/assets/whole-story-13-field.jpg" alt="A young woman gathers fallen barley stalks in a harvest field while a dignified bearded landowner stands nearby watching kindly">
<div class="sg-cap">“And she happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz” (Ruth 2:3).</div>
</div>

<p>To feed them both, Ruth goes out to glean, picking up the grain the harvesters leave behind, which God’s law required farmers to leave for the poor, the foreigner, and the widow (Lev. 19:9–10; Deut. 24:19). Ruth was all three. “And she happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz” (2:3). She “happened” to. The writer smiles as he says it. There are no accidents in God’s world. Boaz is a relative of Naomi’s late husband, and he is a good man. He protects Ruth, feeds her, and blesses her: “a full reward be given you by the LORD, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge!” (2:12).</p>

<p>At Naomi’s urging, Ruth goes to the threshing floor where Boaz is sleeping beside his grain, lies down at his feet, and waits. When he wakes at midnight, she asks him to marry her, using his own words: “Spread your wings over your servant, for you are a redeemer” (3:9). The Hebrew word for “wings” also means the corner of a cloak, and spreading it over a woman was a way of promising marriage and protection (Ezek. 16:8). Boaz calls her “a worthy woman” (3:11) and sends her home before dawn with her honor protected (3:14). Boaz is willing. But there is a closer relative with the first right. Boaz meets him at the town gate, in front of the elders. That man wants the land but backs out when he learns that Ruth comes with it: “I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I impair my own inheritance” (4:6). Any son born to Ruth would carry Mahlon’s name and inherit that land, so he would be paying for property his own family would never keep. Boaz pays the price himself. “Ruth the Moabite, the widow of Mahlon, I have bought to be my wife” (4:10).</p>

<p>The story ends with a baby on Naomi’s lap. The women of the town say, “Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you this day without a redeemer” (4:14). The woman who came home empty is full again. And then the last line turns a family story into world history. The baby’s name was Obed. “He was the father of Jesse, the father of David” (4:17). The Moabite widow became the great-grandmother of Israel’s greatest king.</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">The Turn:</span> Our Kinsman-Redeemer and King</h2>

<p>Judges leaves us longing for a King who will do right. Ruth leaves us longing for a Redeemer who will pay. Both point to the same Person, born about eleven hundred years later in the same little town of Bethlehem, a descendant of Ruth and Boaz (Matt. 1:5–6).</p>

<p>Remember the three things a redeemer had to be. First, a close relative. So the Son of God became one of us. “Since therefore the children share in flesh and blood, he himself likewise partook of the same things” (Heb. 2:14). He is not ashamed to call us his brothers (2:11). Second, able to pay. Our debt was not silver or gold. “You were ransomed… not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb without blemish or spot” (1 Pet. 1:18–19). Third, willing. The nearer relative in Ruth would not risk his own inheritance. Jesus gave up everything. “God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law, to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons” (Gal. 4:4–5).</p>

<p>And he is the King Judges was crying out for. The judges rescued Israel for a few years and died. Jesus rose from the dead, and “he is able to save to the uttermost those who draw near to God through him, since he always lives to make intercession for them” (Heb. 7:25). He beats our enemies, and he changes our hearts, so we stop doing what is right in our own eyes and start loving what is right in his.</p>

<p>Maybe you feel like Naomi: you went out full and came back empty. Or maybe you feel like Ruth, an outsider who has no right to be among God’s people. The book of Ruth is for you. Come the way Ruth came. Leave your old gods behind and say what Ruth said: “your God my God.” Turn from your sin, trust him, and take refuge under his wings. He is your Redeemer, and he is willing.</p>
