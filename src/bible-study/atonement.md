<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Introduction:</span> Why All the Blood?</h2>

<p>Leviticus is the book where most plans to read through the Bible quietly die. You come out of the drama of Exodus, and suddenly you are reading about the fat on a bull’s kidneys, which animals may be eaten, and what to do about mold on a wall. And there is blood everywhere. Bulls, goats, lambs, and birds, slaughtered and burned, day after day. For a modern reader it can feel primitive, even disturbing. Why would God want this?</p>

<p>Start where Leviticus starts. Exodus ended with the glory of God filling the tabernacle so powerfully that even Moses could not go in (Ex. 40:35). Leviticus opens with the very next moment: “The LORD called Moses and spoke to him from the tent of meeting” (Lev. 1:1). God has moved in. Now the question is urgent. How can sinful people live next door to a holy God and not be destroyed? Leviticus is God’s answer. Every sacrifice in it is a picture of the answer that would finally come in Jesus.</p>

<p>This lesson walks through the sacrifices, the holiness that made them necessary, and the most solemn day of Israel’s year, the Day of Atonement. By the end you will see why the New Testament calls Jesus both our High Priest and our sacrifice, and why his work never needs to be repeated.</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Part One:</span> A Life for a Life</h2>

<p>Back in the garden, God told Adam that the day he ate from the forbidden tree he would “surely die” (Gen. 2:17). Paul sums up the whole Bible’s teaching in one line: “the wages of sin is death” (Rom. 6:23). Sin is not a small mistake that a holy God can wave away. It is rebellion against the source of all life, and its result is death.</p>

<p>The sacrifices teach a second truth alongside the first. God, in mercy, would accept a substitute. Watch what an Israelite did when he brought a burnt offering. He chose an animal from his own herd, “a male without blemish,” and brought it to the entrance of the tent. Then “he shall lay his hand on the head of the burnt offering, and it shall be accepted for him to make atonement for him” (Lev. 1:3–4). Then he killed it himself (Lev. 1:5).</p>

<div class="sg-figure sg-small">
<img src="/assets/whole-story-11-altar.jpg" alt="An Israelite man kneels and rests his hand on the head of a young lamb beside a horned altar with fire on it, while a priest in white stands nearby">
<div class="sg-cap">“He shall lay his hand on the head of the burnt offering, and it shall be accepted for him to make atonement for him” (Lev. 1:4).</div>
</div>

<p>Picture it. Your hand on the warm head of an animal you raised. The life that leaves it is the life your sin deserved to lose. That is the meaning of the laid-on hand: this animal stands in my place. Theologians call it <em>substitution</em>, and it is the heart of the whole system. “It is the blood that makes atonement by the life” (Lev. 17:11). Hebrews puts it plainly: “without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness of sins” (Heb. 9:22).</p>

<p>It was costly. A farmer’s best animal was his savings and his livelihood. And it was never meant to be mechanical. The prophets warned that God takes no pleasure in sacrifices offered by people who have no intention of changing (Isa. 1:11–17; Hos. 6:6; Amos 5:21–24). David, after his own great sin, wrote, “The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit; a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise” (Ps. 51:17). The animal on the altar was meant to go with a heart bowed in repentance and trusting God’s promise.</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Part Two:</span> Five Offerings</h2>

<p>Leviticus 1–7 describes five main offerings. You do not need to memorize the details, but it helps to see that they cover the whole of life with God: guilt removed, devotion given, thanks offered, and fellowship enjoyed.</p>

<p>Notice the peace offering. The fat was burned for God and a portion went to the priest, but most of the meat was given back to the worshiper, who ate it with his household “before the LORD” (Lev. 7:15, 31–34; Deut. 12:7). Sacrifice was not only about guilt. It ended at a table. The point of atonement was always fellowship: God and his people at peace, sharing a meal. Christians still eat at that kind of table. In the Lord’s Supper, believers really feed on Christ by faith, not by bringing a new sacrifice but by receiving the one already finished.</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Part Three:</span> Holy, Holy, Holy</h2>

<p>The reason for all this is the holiness of God. Leviticus says it again and again: “You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy” (Lev. 19:2). <em>Holy</em> means set apart, utterly pure, in a class by himself. And Leviticus shows that God’s holiness is not safe to treat casually.</p>

<p>Right at the start of the priests’ work, two of Aaron’s sons, Nadab and Abihu, “offered unauthorized fire before the LORD, which he had not commanded them. And fire came out from before the LORD and consumed them” (Lev. 10:1–2). Moses told Aaron what the LORD had said: “Among those who are near me I will be sanctified, and before all the people I will be glorified” (10:3). Leviticus 16, the chapter on the Day of Atonement, begins by looking back to that day: “The LORD spoke to Moses after the death of the two sons of Aaron, when they drew near before the LORD and died” (16:1). The lesson was burned into Israel’s memory. You cannot walk into God’s presence on your own terms.</p>

<p>Holiness touched every part of ordinary life too. Leviticus is the book that first says, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself” (Lev. 19:18), the command Jesus called the second greatest. A holy God wants a holy people, in worship and in the way they treat one another.</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Part Four:</span> The Day of Atonement</h2>

<p>Once a year, “in the seventh month, on the tenth day of the month” (16:29), all Israel stopped. No one worked. The people were to “afflict” themselves, which means humbling themselves before God, usually by fasting. And the high priest did something he did on no other day. He went behind the veil into the innermost room, the Most Holy Place (Leviticus 16 calls it “the Holy Place inside the veil”), before the mercy seat.</p>

<div class="sg-figure sg-small">
<img src="/assets/whole-story-11-linen.jpg" alt="A barefoot high priest in a plain white linen robe and turban, head bowed, holds a golden censer as incense smoke rises beside a heavy embroidered curtain">
<div class="sg-cap">On this one day the high priest laid aside his garments “for glory and for beauty” (Ex. 28:2) and wore plain white linen (Lev. 16:4).</div>
</div>

<p>Here is how the day unfolded.</p>

<p><strong>He came humbly.</strong> The high priest bathed and put on plain white linen, not his gold and jeweled garments (16:4). On this day he came before God stripped of glory.</p>

<p><strong>He atoned for himself first.</strong> He sacrificed a bull “as a sin offering for himself” and for his household (16:6, 11). The man who stood between God and the people was a sinner too.</p>

<p><strong>He went behind the veil.</strong> He carried a censer of burning coals and two handfuls of incense into the Most Holy Place, so “that the cloud of the incense may cover the mercy seat… so that he does not die” (16:12–13). Then he sprinkled the bull’s blood on the mercy seat and in front of it seven times (16:14).</p>

<p><strong>The goat for the LORD.</strong> Two goats had been chosen by casting lots. The first was killed as a sin offering for the people, and its blood was carried behind the veil and sprinkled on the mercy seat, just like the bull’s (16:15). Remember what was inside the ark: the law Israel had broken. Now the blood of a substitute covered it, in the presence of God.</p>

<p><strong>The goat sent away.</strong> Then came the most unforgettable moment of the day. “Aaron shall lay both his hands on the head of the live goat, and confess over it all the iniquities of the people of Israel, and all their transgressions, all their sins. And he shall put them on the head of the goat and send it away into the wilderness” (16:21). Imagine standing in the crowd as your sins walked out of the camp and disappeared over the horizon. “The goat shall bear all their iniquities on itself to a remote area” (16:22). This is where our word <em>scapegoat</em> comes from: William Tyndale translated the Hebrew as “the scapegoat,” the goat that escapes. The goat is sent “to Azazel” (16:8, 10), a Hebrew word whose exact meaning is debated, but the picture is clear: the sins are carried away, never to return.</p>

<p>The two goats show two sides of one atonement. The first goat died, and its blood answered God’s just anger against sin. The second goat carried the guilt away. Sin is paid for, and sin is removed. David sang about it later: “as far as the east is from the west, so far does he remove our transgressions from us” (Ps. 103:12).</p>

<p>And the result? “For on this day shall atonement be made for you to cleanse you. You shall be clean before the LORD from all your sins” (16:30). It was good news. But it came with a sentence that hung over the whole system: “atonement may be made for the people of Israel <em>once in the year</em>” (16:34). Next year they would have to do it all again. And the year after that. For fifteen centuries. Hebrews calls it “a reminder of sins every year” (Heb. 10:3).</p>

<div class="sg-thread">
<div class="sg-thread-head">Thread Watch</div>
<div class="sg-thread-row"><span class="sg-thread-tag sg-lamb">The Lamb</span> The thread of substitution runs straight from Eden to here. God clothed Adam and Eve with “garments of skins” (Gen. 3:21), which means something died to cover their shame. Abraham told Isaac, “God will provide for himself the lamb” (Gen. 22:8). The Passover lamb’s blood marked the doors (Ex. 12:13). Now every day at the altar and every year behind the veil, a substitute dies. The question keeps growing louder: who is the Lamb God himself will provide?</div>
<div class="sg-thread-row"><span class="sg-thread-tag sg-dwell">God with his people</span> The tent of meeting “dwells with them in the midst of their uncleannesses” (16:16). That is the wonder of Leviticus. God does not move out because his people are unclean. He provides a way to make them clean so he can stay.</div>
</div>

<h3 class="sg-h3">How Were Old Testament Believers Saved?</h3>

<p>If the sacrifices had to be repeated, did they actually work? Leviticus says the worshiper “shall be forgiven” (Lev. 4:31). Yet Hebrews says plainly, “it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins” (Heb. 10:4). Both are true. The forgiveness was real, but the power behind it was never in the animal. An animal cannot truly stand in for a human being. So how were faithful Israelites forgiven?</p>

<p>The same way we are: by grace, through faith in the Savior God promised. The sacrifices were real, God-given signs. When a believing Israelite laid his hand on the animal, he was trusting God’s promise of forgiveness, and that promise was always resting on the sacrifice still to come. The Westminster Confession says the benefits of Christ’s death were given to believers in every age “in and by those promises, types, and sacrifices” that pointed to him (WCF 8.6). Think of it like this. A friend whose word never fails promises to pay your debt in full on a set day. The moment you trust his promise you are free, even though the payment is still coming. The sacrifices were God’s signed promise in advance. The Confession says they were enough to build up believers “in faith in the promised Messiah, by whom they had full remission of sins, and eternal salvation” (WCF 7.5). Paul says that “in his divine forbearance he had passed over former sins,” and the cross shows that God was right to do so (Rom. 3:25–26).</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">The Turn:</span> Once for All</h2>

<p>Read Leviticus 16 and then read Hebrews 9 and 10, and you will see that the New Testament writer had the Day of Atonement open in front of him. Every detail finds its answer in Jesus.</p>

<p>The high priest had to offer a sacrifice for his own sins first. Jesus did not. He is “holy, innocent, unstained, separated from sinners,” and has no need “to offer sacrifices daily, first for his own sins and then for those of the people, since he did this once for all when he offered up himself” (Heb. 7:26–27). He is both the priest and the sacrifice.</p>

<p>The high priest went into a tent made by human hands, carrying the blood of animals. Christ “entered once for all into the holy places, not by means of the blood of goats and calves but by means of his own blood, thus securing an eternal redemption” (Heb. 9:12). He entered “into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf” (9:24).</p>

<p>He is the goat for the LORD. On the cross Jesus bore the punishment our sins deserved, and God’s just anger against our sin fell on him instead of us. Paul’s word for this is <em>propitiation</em>: a sacrifice that turns away God’s wrath by bearing it. God “put forward” Jesus “as a propitiation by his blood, to be received by faith,” so that God might be “just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus” (Rom. 3:25–26). And he is the goat sent away, carrying our sins out of sight. “The LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all” (Isa. 53:6). The bodies of the Day of Atonement sacrifices were burned outside the camp, and “so Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood” (Heb. 13:12).</p>

<p>Look again at the furniture of the tabernacle from Lesson 10. There was a lampstand, a table, two altars, a basin, and an ark. There was no chair for the priest. The only seat in the tabernacle was the mercy seat, and that belonged to God. Nothing in that tent invited a priest to sit down and rest, because his work was never finished. “Every priest stands daily at his service, offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins. But when Christ had offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins, he sat down at the right hand of God” (Heb. 10:11–12). Jesus sat down because the work was finished.</p>

<p>That means something enormous for you. “Where there is forgiveness of these, there is no longer any offering for sin” (Heb. 10:18). You do not need to bring God anything to pay for your sins. No good deeds, no years of feeling bad. Everything that had to be paid has been paid. What God asks is that you lay your hand, so to speak, on the head of the Lamb. Trust him as the one who died in your place, and turn from your sin to him. Faith receives the gift. Repentance always comes with it. “For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God” (2 Cor. 5:21).</p>

<h3 class="sg-h3">Living It: Six Practices for This Week</h3>

<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">1</span><strong>Read the day.</strong> Read Leviticus 16 slowly, then Hebrews 9:11–14 and 10:11–18. Mark every place where Hebrews echoes Leviticus.</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">2</span><strong>Confess specifically.</strong> Aaron confessed “all the iniquities” over the goat. Take time this week to name your sins to God, one by one, and then thank him that Christ has carried them away.</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">3</span><strong>Stop paying.</strong> Is there a sin you keep trying to make up for? Read Hebrews 10:18 aloud. There is no longer any offering for sin.</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">4</span><strong>Take God’s holiness seriously.</strong> Read Leviticus 10:1–3. Ask God to give you reverence when you pray and worship.</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">5</span><strong>Love your neighbor.</strong> Leviticus 19:18 comes from this book. Do one concrete, costly act of love for someone near you.</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">6</span><strong>Memorize Hebrews 10:12.</strong> “But when Christ had offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins, he sat down at the right hand of God.”</div>

<p class="sg-refs"><b>Before Lesson 12:</b> Read Numbers 13–14, Deuteronomy 6, and Joshua 1. Lesson 12 follows Israel through forty years in the wilderness and into the Promised Land.</p>
