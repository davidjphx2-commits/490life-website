<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Introduction:</span> The Lamb God Provided</h2>

<p>In Week 12, Isaac asked his father a question on the way up Mount Moriah: “where is the lamb for a burnt offering?” Abraham answered, “God will provide for himself the lamb” (Gen. 22:7–8). Ever since, the story has been full of lambs. The Passover lamb whose blood marked the doors in Egypt (Week 16). The lambs on the altar, morning and evening, for well over a thousand years. The goat for the LORD on the Day of Atonement (Week 22). The Servant in Isaiah “like a lamb that is led to the slaughter” (Isa. 53:7). And John the Baptist pointing at Jesus: “Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!” (John 1:29).</p>

<p>These two weeks are about the day the Lamb was offered. It is the center of the whole Bible. The four Gospels spend roughly a third of their pages on the last week of Jesus’ life. Jesus told his disciples again and again that “the Son of Man must suffer many things and be rejected… and be killed, and after three days rise again” (Mark 8:31). He “set his face to go to Jerusalem” (Luke 9:51), knowing exactly what waited there.</p>

<p>On our timeline, Jesus was crucified in Jerusalem at Passover, around AD 30 or 33 (scholars debate the exact year). We will walk through his last night and his death, and then ask the most important question in this whole study: what happened on the cross, and was it for you?</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Part One:</span> The Last Supper</h2>

<p>On the night before he died, Jesus ate the Passover meal with his twelve disciples in an upstairs room in Jerusalem. For well over a thousand years, Israel had eaten this meal to remember the lamb whose blood saved them in Egypt. Now Jesus gave it a new meaning:</p>



<div class="sg-verse"><span class="sg-ref">Luke 22:19–20</span> &nbsp;“And he took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it and gave it to them, saying, ‘This is my body, which is given for you. Do this in remembrance of me.’ And likewise the cup after they had eaten, saying, ‘This cup that is poured out for you is the new covenant in my blood.’”</div>

<p>Jesus was saying: I am the Passover Lamb. My body will be broken, my blood will be poured out, and it will be for you. “For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed” (1 Cor. 5:7). And with his blood he would establish the new covenant God promised through Jeremiah in the ashes of Jerusalem (Week 34). Christians still eat this meal, the Lord’s Supper. It is a memorial, but more than a memorial. In it, as the Westminster Shorter Catechism says, the worthy receivers “are, not after a corporal and carnal manner, but by faith, made partakers of his body and blood, with all his benefits, to their spiritual nourishment, and growth in grace” (WSC 96).</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">Part Two:</span> The Cup in the Garden</h2>

<p>After supper, Jesus walked out to an olive grove called Gethsemane. He “began to be greatly distressed and troubled. And he said to them, ‘My soul is very sorrowful, even to death’” (Mark 14:33–34). Falling to the ground, he prayed: “Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will” (14:36).</p>

<div class="sg-figure sg-small">
<img src="/assets/whole-story-21-garden.jpg" alt="At night in an olive grove, Jesus kneels beside a large rock praying while three disciples sleep in the background and torches approach from the city">
<div class="sg-cap">“Yet not what I will, but what you will” (Mark 14:36).</div>
</div>

<p>What was the cup? In the Old Testament, “the cup” is a picture of God’s wrath against sin. Isaiah speaks of Jerusalem drinking “from the hand of the LORD the cup of his wrath” (Isa. 51:17), and God told Jeremiah, “Take from my hand this cup of the wine of wrath” (Jer. 25:15). The nails were real, but they were not the deepest thing he faced. He was facing the full weight of God’s judgment against the sins of his people. And he chose to drink it.</p>

<p>Soldiers came with torches and arrested him, and “they all left him and fled” (Mark 14:50). Through the night he was put on trial. The high priest asked him, “Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?” Jesus answered, “I am” (14:61–62), and they condemned him for blasphemy. In the morning they brought him to the Roman governor, Pontius Pilate. Pilate offered the crowd a choice: he would release one prisoner, Jesus or a rebel and murderer named Barabbas (Luke 23:19). The crowd chose Barabbas. “So Pilate, wishing to satisfy the crowd, released for them Barabbas, and having scourged Jesus, he delivered him to be crucified” (Mark 15:15). The guilty man walked free, and the innocent one went to the cross in his place.</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">The Turn:</span> Not What I Will</h2>

<p>In a garden, the first Adam reached for what God had forbidden. In another garden, the last Adam fell to the ground and prayed, “Not what I will, but what you will.” Jesus knew what was in the cup, and he did not turn back.</p>

<p>Hebrews says that “although he was a son, he learned obedience through what he suffered” (Heb. 5:8), and Paul says that “by the one man’s obedience the many will be made righteous” (Rom. 5:19). Every step from the upper room to the garden was obedience offered for his people. When your own will fights God’s, you are not praying alone. The Son prayed that prayer before you and kept it all the way to the cross. Next week we stand at the foot of that cross and ask what happened there.</p>

<h3 class="sg-h3">Living It: Three Practices for This Week</h3>

<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">1</span><strong>Watch him keep going.</strong> Read Mark 14:32–50 slowly. Mark each place Jesus could have turned back, and thank him that he did not.</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">2</span><strong>Come to the table.</strong> If you are a baptized believer and a member of a church, come to the Lord’s Supper with fresh gratitude for what it shows and gives. If you are not there yet, talk with a pastor about what it means to come.</div>
<div class="sg-principle"><span class="sg-pnum">3</span><strong>Memorize Mark 14:36.</strong> “Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will.”</div>

<p class="sg-refs"><b>Before Week 42:</b> Read Mark 15 and Isaiah 52:13–53:12. Week 42 stands at the cross.</p>

<h2 class="sg-section"><span class="sg-num">For Discussion</span> </h2>

<ol>
<li>Jesus called the bread his body and the cup the new covenant in his blood. What does the Lord’s Supper show us about the cross?</li>
<li>What was “the cup” Jesus asked the Father to remove? Why did he drink it anyway?</li>
<li>Barabbas went free while Jesus was condemned. How is that a picture of what Jesus does for us?</li>
<li>The disciples “all left him and fled” (Mark 14:50). What does Jesus’ faithfulness to people who abandoned him say to you?</li>
</ol>
